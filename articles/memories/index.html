Memories

On hash maps, noodles, old songs and Diwali.

Jatin Garg

10 November 2026

Some days stay

We remember some memories, some dates, some events more strongly than others, like the day your sibling was born, or the day you got your first job, or how you used to get so excited about Diwali as a kid. We all remember these events very vividly compared to some day when we got irritated by Uber not working properly, or just some random bad morning.

You meet a close friend and spend the whole day with them: eating, walking, talking about all sorts of things, from geopolitics to those “deep” talks. And on the other hand, there’s yesterday’s lunch chat. With some people, I remember the exact dates I met them, the exact food we ate, the exact clothes I wore and they wore, everything, very vividly. Does that mean I have a good memory? No, not really, because I can’t even remember what I ate a few days ago, or what I chatted about at lunch yesterday.

Novelty

This selectivity is not so simple, and we can try many ways to explain it. One is “novelty”: how novel that incident was. You don’t get to meet close friends every day (a bit unfortunate, right?), and that makes it much more novel than the everyday lunch and the everyday casual chats. Our brain likes novelty.

One really cool way to imagine this is using hash maps. A hash map takes a key, “hashes” it to some index, and stores the value(s) at that index. Of course, collisions can happen, and different implementations tackle them differently, but a unique index is still the most optimal. The hippocampus is sort of the place in the brain that handles our memories of events: it consolidates short-term memories into long-term ones. The way it does this is pretty fascinating, and crazily similar to hash maps. Suppose time is not continuous, but a very fast stream of “events”, or “ticks”. Each tick fires up some neurons in different places in our brain, at different intensities, and the hippocampus takes that as the input to get the hashed index. Everyday stuff keeps piling up into the same bucket(s), but a meeting with someone close forms a unique index and takes a special place in our memories. In layman’s terms, it’s an O(1) look-up vs O(N) or O(log N) look-ups through a crowded bucket.

This is closer to real neuroscience than it sounds. One well-known theory says the hippocampus doesn’t keep the memory itself, only an index to it: pointers to the pieces, which stay spread across the rest of the brain [1]. We’ll come back to those pieces.

Emotional significance

Novelty is not the only factor. The emotional significance we attach to our memories, the same E from Attachment, is also important. Suppose we met more frequently (in a parallel world, of course), at similar places, doing similar activities. There’s a good chance you would still remember a lot of the details very vividly, because of the emotional significance attached to those particular ticks. What is emotional significance? It is not the intensity of the emotions experienced in that moment. It’s how much that moment matters to us emotionally, which influences how strongly we attend to it, react to it, and potentially remember it. In the brain, the amygdala, which handles emotions, helps decide this: it tells the hippocampus which moments to hold on to more strongly [2].

Suppose you ate noodles with them. The next time you see noodles, your brain will bring that moment back right in front of you, and silly you will instantly send them a pic of the noodles. That’s because we attached emotional significance to that person and that moment, so the food we ate also got our attention. Months later, listening to a song that was played that day would instantly make you remember everything vividly.

Remembering is rebuilding

There is one more factor: remembering memories repeatedly, reconstructing them in our mind, over and over. Suppose you met someone you really like. The next day you keep remembering the moments, getting all giggly, feeling those weird butterflies in your stomach. We keep “reconstructing” that memory over and over, and that leaves a strong print on our mind. Why did I use “reconstructing”? Because we can’t relive memories exactly how they happened (unless, of course, Doraemon comes to life). The parts of a memory are scattered across different places in the brain, and the hippocampus helps link them. Whenever we try to remember something, the same neurons that fired at that moment tend to fire again, to give us a similar experience, but still not the exact one. In one study, neurons in the hippocampus that fired while people watched a short film clip fired again when they later recalled it, just before they said what they remembered [3].

Sometimes, because we remember a memory so many times, we override some parts and fill the gaps subconsciously. So even the vivid ones aren’t proof of a good memory.

Remembering the future

Suppose you plan to meet again, maybe in a month. That event hasn’t even happened yet, but subconsciously we start imagining it: where we will meet, what we will eat. We use our memories to create an event in our brain that hasn’t happened yet. So memory isn’t useful only for revisiting the past; it also contributes to imagining and planning for the future. People with damage to the hippocampus, who struggle to remember their past, also struggle to imagine new experiences: asked to picture simple everyday scenes, they could only describe scattered fragments [4].

Memories aren’t just records of what happened to us; they influence how we experience the things that happen afterwards. We use past memories as feedback to do “better” at future events, like replacing the bitter coffee with a chocolate shake next time.

The old us

You go to the same restaurant you went to with someone close, but with different people. That restaurant won’t feel the same to you. The food and the place are the same, but it still won’t feel all happy happy. In fact, compared to a restaurant you have never been to before, you might feel a bit negative, because you start remembering the previous time you were there, how happy you were, and you feel nostalgia. Though it depends on the emotional significance you attach to that person and that memory. The same food may not taste as good as it did in the past, because taste is not just a sensory input from the tongue. What we call taste is put together from the tongue, the nose and various other signals, including our surroundings [5].

Memories don’t just help us relive the past or shape the future; they also alter the present we live in. Remember Diwali when we were in school: all excited about the holidays, the crackers, the good food, or, for someone like me, just watching TV quietly in the morning. Why doesn’t Diwali feel the same now? Same festival, same people, same food (maybe even better, technically), and the freedom to buy whatever gift we want. But something changed. Maybe it’s us, or the circumstances, that Diwali doesn’t feel the same. It starts feeling like nostalgia: we remember the happy moments, but also get sad that we can’t physically relive them. Perhaps that’s the curse of memories. Maybe it’s novelty again: for that kid, every Diwali was new and got a bucket of its own, and now they start piling into the same one. Maybe we don’t miss the old Diwali; we miss the old us. We are probably not nostalgic about Diwali, we are nostalgic about that child who saw the world a bit differently.

But there is something pretty interesting here. What if, ten years from now, we feel the same nostalgia for this Diwali? Perhaps we are already living through memories that our future selves will wish they could relive.

Memories let us revisit moments we can never truly return to, while sometimes making us overlook the moments we’re living right now.
