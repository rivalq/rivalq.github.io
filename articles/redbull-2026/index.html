Abstract

The 2026 Formula 1 rules made roughly half of a car's peak power electric and placed it under a per-lap energy budget and a speed-dependent limit. I study how Red Bull Racing closed its early-season deficit using — sessions, — timed laps and — passes along full-throttle straights. Laps are aligned by projecting car positions onto a reference racing line and reconciled to official lap times. Race pace is corrected for fuel, tyre age, compound and dirty air. Every comparison carries a 95% bootstrap interval over rounds. Red Bull's qualifying gap to pole fell from — to —, and about half of its gain on Mercedes came on straights. The straight-line gain is a corner-exit gain: Red Bull's speed-trap reading fell relative to the field, and it accelerates less than Mercedes once on a straight. The hypothesis that Red Bull won time by spending its electric energy better is consistent with some of the data but not supported by the entry-controlled test. Mercedes' straight-line advantage in races faded. A difference-in-differences test against Ferrari-powered cars finds no immediate effect of the 1 June compression-ratio test.

Keywords: Formula 1, telemetry, energy management, power units, difference-in-differences, FastF1

Introduction

In March Red Bull qualified more than a second off pole at Suzuka. By October it had taken consecutive poles at Sepang and Singapore. The paddock explanation is that Red Bull "found straight-line speed" and is now quicker than Mercedes on the straights. A second claim is that the FIA's compression-ratio test of 1 June took away a Mercedes engine advantage. Both are hard to test from speed traps: in 2026 a car can lose fifty kilometres per hour on a straight while its driver keeps the throttle pressed (Section 4.3).

This note asks four questions. Where on the lap did Red Bull find its time? How much of its straight-line gain is its own, and how much is Mercedes slowing down? What does the shape of the gain say about its cause? And did the compression-ratio test leave a measurable mark?

Data and methods

Data

The data are every 2026 session available on 10 October, from Australia to Singapore qualifying, downloaded from Formula 1's live-timing archive with FastF1 [6]. They cover lap timing, car telemetry (speed, RPM, gear, throttle, brake) at about 4 Hz, car positions at about 4 Hz, weather and race-control messages. The 2026 Bahrain Grand Prix was held at Sepang and is treated as Sepang throughout. The public feed carries no battery, Overtake or wing-state channel, and the legacy DRS field is zero for all of 2026.

Exclusions. Monaco is excluded from every straight-line and race measure: several cars had power-unit failures, it has no Straight-Mode zones, and its race position feed stops halfway. Hungary's race position data are degraded, so it is excluded from race straight-line measures. Only laps on slick tyres are used, and qualifying uses each team's fastest non-deleted lap with valid telemetry.

Alignment on the racing line

For each circuit I build a reference line 𝐫(s) from the position samples of a clean pole lap, densified to 1 m. Every position sample 𝐩i is mapped to the track coordinate

si= argmins∈[0,L) ‖𝐩i−𝐫(s)‖ (2)

and car-data samples inherit s by interpolation in time. Each driver's clock offset between timing and telemetry is estimated from the line crossings it implies (it reached 21 s in the rain-delayed Sepang race). Laps are rejected when they leave the racing line, when their reconstructed duration differs from the official lap time by more than 0.25 s, or when a residual clock offset remains. That removes — of timed qualifying and race laps. The accepted laps have a median duration error of — (99th percentile —), and each is then rescaled so that its time matches the official lap time exactly.

Straights, corners and clean air

A circuit's straights are the full-throttle zones of its reference lap that are at least 200 m long. A zone that runs through the start/finish line is one straight, measured from the end of the previous lap. Straights are timed over their first 90%, which keeps the comparison clear of braking points. Corners are classed by reference apex speed: slow below 120 km/h, medium 120–200 km/h, fast above that. The gap g to the car ahead is the time since any other car crossed the line. A lap is clean when g>2 s, and laps with no measurable gap are left out.

Race-pace model

For each race, green-flag laps on slick tyres, excluding the first lap, in- and out-laps, and laps more than 4% off the median, are fitted by ordinary least squares:

td,ℓ= αd+γc+βcad,ℓ +φnℓ +δ1𝟙[g<1] +δ2𝟙[1≤g≤2] +εd,ℓ (3)

where d is the driver, c the compound, a tyre age and n the lap number. The lap term φ absorbs fuel burn and track evolution together; only their sum is identified. A team's pace is its better driver's prediction at common conditions. The model is descriptive: it assumes the same compound effects for every team, and the driver term mixes car and driver. Comparing against the field, with uncertainty

Each team's measure yT is taken relative to the field median y~ in each round and averaged over rounds 1–5 and rounds 6–17:

ΔT= ⟨yT−y~⟩R6–17 − ⟨yT−y~⟩R1–5 (4)

A negative ΔT means team T became quicker relative to everyone else. The boundary follows the FIA's first engine-review period (rounds 1–5); it was not chosen from the data, and it is not pre-registered. Intervals are 95% percentile bootstraps that resample whole rounds; with five or six rounds per phase they are wide, and many include zero.

Discussion

Each explanation below is stated as a hypothesis with what the data predict and what they show.

  1. Red Bull leaves corners faster. Red Bull's straight-line gain is carried in from the preceding corner, and its medium-speed corner losses halved. This fits the Miami and Austria packages and a more driveable car. Supported.
  2. Red Bull-Ford spends its energy better. If so, Red Bull should accelerate harder along straights at matched entry speed. It does not, and its trap speed fell. The data cannot observe deployment directly, so the hypothesis is not supported but not excluded.
  3. Mercedes lost its straight-line edge. Its race straight-line change against the field is the largest of any team, with an interval that just includes zero. Probable; cause unclear.
  4. The compression-ratio test cost Mercedes power. No measurable effect in the races right after it; the later drop matches Ferrari's engine upgrade and hits Red Bull-Ford too; the strongest-case analysis finds a consistent but statistically fragile acceleration loss. Not established; an effect below about 13 hp cannot be excluded.
  5. The rotating rear wing helped on the straights. Spa, without it, was Red Bull's worst trap deficit; a single event is weak evidence. Plausible.

Limitations

Energy use is inferred from speed traces, because the public feed carries no state of charge, Overtake state or wing position. The phases contain five or six rounds on different circuits, so track mix is controlled only through comparisons with the field or a control group in the same round. Qualifying uses each team's best lap, which mixes in driver effects and changes of driver (Lawson replaced Hadjar at three events). The race-pace model is descriptive and assumes common compound effects. The compression-ratio test has one change date and several overlapping confounders: the Miami energy rules, three other manufacturers' engine upgrades and Mercedes' own aerodynamic changes. A "supported" verdict means the data fit and nothing contradicts them, not proof.
